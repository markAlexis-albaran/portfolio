## yumm

Optimizing an affiliate product page for trust and conversion
Media / Publishing · 2024

Source: `00 - Affiliate Marketing Product.pdf` in Google Drive, folder "UX Case Studies". The publisher stays anonymous as yumm; the photo credit naming the real publisher is covered in the images.

### The problem
yumm is a German food and cooking publisher that earns money through affiliate links in its buying guides. Its electric grill guide brought in search traffic from people with one question: which electric grill should I buy? But every product card looked the same, buy buttons appeared before there was any reason to trust them, and on mobile the guide turned into a long scroll. People skimmed, hesitated at the links and left before reaching the lower-ranked grills, which cost clicks and affiliate revenue.

### Research
I combined a heuristic evaluation against Nielsen's heuristics with interviews and a usability test with 25 participants, most of whom cook every day. They completed three tasks: find the best grill for a family of four, find the cheapest reliable option, and choose one they'd buy themselves. The baseline was a 6.2% click-through rate on buy buttons, 24% of visitors scrolling past three quarters of the page, and €0.18 revenue per visitor. Participants liked the editorial tone and detailed product information. The problems were hierarchy (they couldn't tell the best, budget and premium picks apart), trust (the links felt like advertising) and length on mobile.

Participants: 25
Task success: 48% before, 68% after. The PDF has the two swapped (68% → 48%) and lists 68% next to the other baseline numbers.

### The insight
People trusted the writing but not the ranking: the page asked them to click before it showed them why.

### What was made and why

**A quick comparison at the top.** I placed a comparison module near the top of the guide, with color-coded badges (Best Overall, Best Value) and checkboxes to compare two grills side by side in a spec table, instead of a long list of identical cards. Participants couldn't tell which grill was best, cheapest or premium, and asked to see the differences without reading every section.

**A sticky "Jump to Best Price" button on mobile.** A persistent button jumps straight to the top pick and its price, instead of buy buttons that only live inside each card. On mobile those buttons scrolled out of view, and participants said they had to scroll too far before they could decide anything.

**Compact product cards.** Each card now shows the badge, product, price, one main buy button and the rating, with other sellers and technical data folded into accordions, instead of everything expanded at once. Large blocks of text made the page hard to scan and slowed decisions.

### Outcome
Task success went from 48% to 68%. Click-through on buy buttons went from 6.2% to 12.5%, mobile conversion from 2.1% to 4.3%, and revenue per visitor from €0.18 to €0.36. More people reached the end of the guide (scroll depth past 75% went from 24% to 40%), and time on page rose from 1:42 to 2:35. Some users still questioned the ranking, so next I'd show the testing criteria and scoring method, and add picks by need, like best for balconies, for families or under €100.
